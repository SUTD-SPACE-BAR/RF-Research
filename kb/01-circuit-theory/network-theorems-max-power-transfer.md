# Network theorems and maximum power transfer

## Questions this page answers
- How do KCL and KVL turn a circuit into equations I can solve?
- What is nodal analysis and why do simulators use it?
- What are the Thévenin and Norton equivalents and how do I find them?
- When can I use superposition (and why can't I superpose powers)?
- What is the maximum power transfer theorem, and what is "available power"?
- Why is the load impedance for maximum power the *complex conjugate* of the source impedance?
- Does conjugate matching really waste half the power? Is a matched system only 50 % efficient?
- Why isn't a power amplifier conjugate-matched to its transistor? What is a load-line match?
- Why isn't an LNA conjugate-matched either — what is noise matching?
- If my LoRa module wants 50 Ω, is it "conjugate matched" to the antenna?

## TL;DR
- **KCL** (currents into a node sum to zero) and **KVL** (voltages round a loop sum to zero) are the only laws besides element laws. **Nodal analysis** writes KCL at each node as $\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}$ — the formulation SPICE uses (modified nodal analysis).
- Any linear two-terminal network is equivalent, at its terminals and at one frequency, to $V_{th}$ in series with $Z_{th}$ (Thévenin) or $I_N=V_{th}/Z_{th}$ in parallel with $Z_{th}$ (Norton).
- **Superposition** works for voltages and currents in linear circuits, **never for power**.
- **Maximum power transfer:** a source $V_s$, $Z_s=R_s+jX_s$ delivers its **available power** $P_{av}=|V_s|^2/(8R_s)$ (peak phasor) to a load only when $Z_L=Z_s^*$. Any other load receives $P_L=P_{av}(1-|\Gamma_p|^2)$ with $\Gamma_p=(Z_L-Z_s^*)/(Z_L+Z_s)$.
- At the conjugate match, **half of the power generated by the Thévenin source is dissipated in $R_s$** — but $R_s$ is a *model*. It is only a real resistor when the source really is a resistor (a signal generator's 50 Ω, an antenna's radiation resistance). A matched system is not "50 % efficient" in general.
- **Power amplifiers are load-line matched**, not conjugate matched: the optimum load is set by voltage and current limits, $R_{opt}\approx(V_{DD}-V_{knee})^2/(2P_{out})$. For 2 W from a 5 V stage that is ≈5 Ω; conjugate matching to a 200 Ω output resistance would give only ~50 mW.
- **LNAs are noise matched**: the source reflection coefficient is set to $\Gamma_{opt}$, which in general differs from $S_{11}^*$. In the page's example, noise match gives NF 0.50 dB with 2.3 dB input mismatch; gain match gives 0 dB mismatch but NF 2.06 dB.
- "50 Ω" interfaces are a *standard reference*, not a conjugate match. A LoRa module's "50 Ω output" means it was designed to deliver rated power into 50 Ω, not that its internal source impedance is 50 Ω.

## Intuition (no math)
KCL is conservation of charge: charge can't pile up at a wire junction, so what flows in flows out. KVL is conservation of energy for a charge going round a loop and returning to where it started — it gains and loses the same amount of potential. Everything else in linear circuit analysis is bookkeeping built on these two statements plus the element laws.

Thévenin's theorem says a "black box" of sources and linear parts, viewed from two terminals, can't be distinguished from one voltage source behind one impedance. You measure (or compute) the open-circuit voltage, and the impedance you see looking back in with the sources switched off. This is how we treat a transmitter output, a sensor, an antenna in receive mode (open-circuit voltage from the incident field, radiation + loss resistance as $Z_{th}$), or a filter's output port.

Maximum power transfer is a tug-of-war. A tiny load resistance draws a big current but has almost no voltage across it; a huge one has the full voltage but almost no current. Power $VI$ peaks in between, when the load equals the source resistance. If the source also has reactance, the load should carry the *opposite* reactance so the two cancel and the current is as large as possible — that's the conjugate.

The "50 % efficiency" confusion arises from forgetting what $R_s$ represents. If you connect a 50 Ω signal generator to a 50 Ω load, yes, the generator's internal resistor gets as hot as the load. But for a power amplifier the "source resistance" is not a resistor that burns power: it is the small-signal slope of the transistor's output curve. Its losses are set by how the transistor is biased and switched, not by the $R_s$ in a Thévenin model. And for a PA the real limit is not "how do I extract the most power from this fixed source" but "how do I get the most power out without exceeding the supply voltage and the maximum current" — a different optimisation, with a different answer.

## The math

### Level 1 — the toolkit

**KCL:** $\sum_k i_k=0$ at every node. **KVL:** $\sum_k v_k=0$ around every closed loop. In phasor form the same holds for complex amplitudes $I_k$, $V_k$.

**Nodal analysis.** Pick a ground node; unknowns are node voltages $V_1\dots V_n$. KCL at node $i$: sum of admittance-weighted voltage differences = injected source current. In matrix form
$$\mathbf{Y}\,\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I},\qquad Y_{ii}=\sum(\text{admittances touching }i),\quad Y_{ij}=-(\text{admittance between }i\text{ and }j).$$
Voltage sources are converted to Norton form, or handled by adding their currents as extra unknowns (**modified nodal analysis**, the basis of SPICE). **Mesh analysis** is the dual: loop currents and $\mathbf{Z}\,\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{V}$; convenient for planar circuits with many series elements.

**Thévenin/Norton.** $V_{th}=V_{oc}$ (open-circuit terminal voltage), $I_N=I_{sc}$ (short-circuit current), $Z_{th}=V_{oc}/I_{sc}$ — or, with all *independent* sources zeroed (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened), the impedance seen at the terminals. Dependent sources (transistor models) must *not* be zeroed; use a test source instead.

**Superposition.** In a linear network the response to several independent sources is the sum of the responses to each alone. Because power is quadratic, $P\ne P_1+P_2$ (unless the two responses are orthogonal, e.g. at different frequencies, where average powers do add).

**Maximum power transfer (resistive).** Load current $I=V_s/(R_s+R_L)$, load power $P_L=\tfrac12|I|^2R_L$ (peak phasors). Setting $dP_L/dR_L=0$ gives $R_L=R_s$ and
$$P_{L,\max}=P_{av}=\frac{|V_s|^2}{8R_s}\quad(\text{peak } V_s)\qquad\text{or}\quad\frac{V_{s,\text{rms}}^2}{4R_s}.$$

### Level 2 — the conjugate match, available power and mismatch

With $Z_s=R_s+jX_s$ and $Z_L=R_L+jX_L$:
$$P_L=\frac{|V_s|^2}{2}\,\frac{R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^2+(X_s+X_L)^2}.$$
For any $R_L$ the denominator is minimised by $X_L=-X_s$; then the resistive result gives $R_L=R_s$. Hence $Z_L=Z_s^*$ and $P_L=P_{av}$. The normalised delivered power is the **mismatch factor**
$$M=\frac{P_L}{P_{av}}=\frac{4R_sR_L}{|Z_s+Z_L|^2}=1-|\Gamma_p|^2,\qquad \Gamma_p=\frac{Z_L-Z_s^*}{Z_L+Z_s},$$
the **power-wave reflection coefficient** of Kurokawa. When $Z_s=Z_0$ is real this reduces to the familiar $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$ and mismatch loss $-10\log_{10}(1-|\Gamma|^2)$ ([reflections, VSWR](../03-transmission-lines-matching/reflections-vswr-return-loss-mismatch.md)). All three forms of $M$ are verified numerically for random complex impedances in `tools/verify/circuits_power_transfer.py`.

**Efficiency.** With a resistive Thévenin source, the fraction of generated power that reaches the load is
$$\eta=\frac{R_L}{R_s+R_L},$$
so $\eta=50\%$ at $R_L=R_s$, approaching 100 % only as $R_L\gg R_s$ — at which point $P_L\to0$ relative to $P_{av}$. Maximum *power* and maximum *efficiency* are different optima (Jacobi's law). Power utilities and batteries operate with $R_L\gg R_s$ (efficiency); weak-signal RF receivers operate at $R_L\approx R_s$ (power — every picowatt counts and the "loss" in $R_s$ is fictitious or unavoidable).

**Lossless matching networks preserve available power.** A lossless two-port between source and load cannot increase $P_{av}$; it only changes which impedance the source "sees". Designing a matching network is choosing a lossless transformation that makes $\Gamma_p=0$ at the frequencies of interest ([matching](../03-transmission-lines-matching/impedance-matching-lumped-and-distributed.md)).

### Level 3 — why real RF stages are deliberately *not* conjugate matched

**(a) Power amplifiers: load-line (power) match.** A transistor stage is not a linear Thévenin source with a fixed $V_s$: the output swing is limited by the supply ($V_{DD}$), the knee voltage $V_{knee}$ below which the device leaves saturation/active region, and a maximum current $I_{max}$. For a class-A/B stage the fundamental output voltage amplitude is at most $V_{DD}-V_{knee}$ and the fundamental current amplitude at most $\approx I_{max}/2$. Maximising $P=\tfrac12 V_1I_1$ under both limits gives the **Cripps load-line** optimum
$$R_{opt}=\frac{V_{DD}-V_{knee}}{I_{max}/2}\approx\frac{(V_{DD}-V_{knee})^2}{2P_{out}}.$$
The transistor's small-signal output resistance (hundreds of ohms or more) does not appear at all. With $V_{DD}=5$ V, $V_{knee}=0.5$ V and $P_{out}=2$ W: $R_{opt}=4.5^2/4=5.1\ \Omega$. Into 50 Ω the same stage is voltage-limited to 203 mW; into a "conjugate match" of 200 Ω, 51 mW (script §5). That is why a 2 W LoRa module (e.g. EBYTE E22-400T33D class) contains a matching network transforming its 50 Ω port down to a few ohms, and why its output impedance looking back in is generally *not* 50 Ω. In practice $R_{opt}$ plus a reactive part is found by **load-pull**, and harmonic terminations are added for efficiency classes (AB, E, F) — see [power amplifiers](../06-rf-circuits/power-amplifiers.md).

**(b) Low-noise amplifiers: noise match.** A noisy two-port's noise figure depends on the source reflection coefficient:
$$F=F_{min}+\frac{4R_n}{Z_0}\,\frac{|\Gamma_s-\Gamma_{opt}|^2}{(1-|\Gamma_s|^2)\,|1+\Gamma_{opt}|^2},$$
with device noise parameters $F_{min}$, $R_n$, $\Gamma_{opt}$. The conjugate (gain) match $\Gamma_s=S_{11}^*$ (unilateral case) is generally a different point. For the hypothetical device in the script ($F_{min}=0.5$ dB, $R_n=15\ \Omega$, $\Gamma_{opt}=0.35\angle55^\circ$, $S_{11}=0.80\angle-40^\circ$): noise match → NF 0.50 dB and 2.3 dB of input mismatch loss; gain match → 0 dB mismatch but NF 2.06 dB; plain 50 Ω → NF 0.86 dB. Techniques such as inductive source degeneration move $S_{11}^*$ towards $\Gamma_{opt}$ so both can be approximately satisfied ([LNA design](../06-rf-circuits/lna-design.md), [noise figure](../07-noise-linearity/noise-figure-and-friis.md)).

**(c) Other deliberate mismatches.** Oscillators need a *negative*-resistance condition, not a match; filters are designed for specified source/load terminations; antennas on small platforms may be matched for bandwidth rather than peak transfer; and a PA's output network may trade 0.5 dB of power for linearity (ACPR) or harmonic suppression.

**(d) Receive antennas.** A receiving antenna's Thévenin model is $V_{oc}$ behind $Z_A=R_{rad}+R_{loss}+jX_A$. At conjugate match half of the power extracted from the wave is re-radiated (scattered) by $R_{rad}$ — here the "50 % loss" is physically real, and it is fundamental (the antenna's effective aperture already accounts for it) ([antenna fundamentals](../08-antennas/antenna-fundamentals.md)).

## Design equations & rules of thumb

| Quantity | Equation | When valid | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nodal equations | $\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}$ | linear, one frequency | SPICE uses MNA |
| Thévenin impedance | $Z_{th}=V_{oc}/I_{sc}$ | linear | zero only *independent* sources |
| Available power | $P_{av}=|V_s|^2/(8R_s)$ (peak) | $R_s>0$ | 1 V peak EMF, 50 Ω → 2.5 mW = +4.0 dBm |
| Mismatch factor | $M=4R_sR_L/|Z_s+Z_L|^2=1-|\Gamma_p|^2$ | any complex $Z_s,Z_L$ | $M=1$ iff $Z_L=Z_s^*$ |
| Efficiency with resistive source | $\eta=R_L/(R_s+R_L)$ | $R_s$ is a real resistor | 50 % at match |
| PA optimum load | $R_{opt}\approx(V_{DD}-V_{knee})^2/(2P_{out})$ | class A/AB, fundamental | 2 W, 5 V, 0.5 V knee → ≈5 Ω; 100 mW, 3.3 V, 0.3 V knee → ≈45 Ω |
| Noise figure vs source | $F=F_{min}+\frac{4R_n}{Z_0}\frac{|\Gamma_s-\Gamma_{opt}|^2}{(1-|\Gamma_s|^2)|1+\Gamma_{opt}|^2}$ | linear two-port | $R_n$ small ⇒ NF tolerant of mismatch |

## Worked examples

### 1. Thévenin equivalent of a resistive network
Source 10 V with 100 Ω into node 1; 200 Ω from node 1 to ground; 50 Ω from node 1 to node 2; 300 Ω from node 2 to ground; load at node 2.
- Open circuit: node 1 sees 100 Ω source, 200 Ω, and (50 + 300) Ω: solving gives $V_{oc}=4.800$ V.
- Short circuit current at node 2: $I_{sc}=57.14$ mA. So $R_{th}=84.0\ \Omega$.
- Check by killing the source: $300\parallel\big(50+(100\parallel200)\big)=300\parallel116.7=84.0\ \Omega$ ✓.
- With $R_L=84\ \Omega$ the load receives 68.57 mW, identical from the full nodal solve and from the Thévenin model (script §1). $P_{av}=4.8^2/(4\cdot84)=68.57$ mW ✓ (rms/DC form).

### 2. Mismatch between a signal generator and a load
A 50 Ω generator set to −10 dBm (available power) drives an unmatched load of 25 Ω. $M=4\cdot50\cdot25/75^2=0.889$, i.e. the load receives −10.5 dBm (0.51 dB mismatch loss). Equivalent: $|\Gamma|=1/3$, VSWR 2.0.

### 3. Why a 2 W transmitter wants ≈5 Ω, not 50 Ω
As derived above and in script §5: 5.1 Ω for 2 W at 5 V with 0.5 V knee. The output matching network must transform 50 Ω down by ≈10:1. At 433 MHz a single L-section doing this has $Q=\sqrt{50/5.1-1}=2.97$ — low enough to be broadband and tolerant ([resonance and Q](resonance-and-q.md)). The same arithmetic explains why higher-power PAs run at higher supply voltage: $R_{opt}$ scales with $V^2/P$.

### 4. Noise match vs gain match
See Level 3(b): the "best-looking" $S_{11}$ is not the lowest noise figure. An LNA's datasheet usually quotes NF and gain for a recommended match that is a compromise between the two.

## Practical notes
- **Signal generators and VNAs** really are close to 50 Ω Thévenin sources (a 50 Ω resistor or a leveled source with 50 Ω source match). For those, "available power" is the dial reading.
- **LoRa modules and transceiver ICs** specify output power *into 50 Ω* (after their internal matching). The SX126x datasheet specifies the chip with a specific external matching network; the module maker (EBYTE etc.) has done this for you, which is why you should not add "matching" between the module and a good 50 Ω antenna unless you have measured a problem ([LoRa modules](../15-lora-ism-radios/lora-modules-ebyte-rfm-heltec.md)).
- **Measuring a PA's output impedance with a VNA** while it is off, or small-signal, tells you nothing about its large-signal optimum load. Load-pull or the manufacturer's recommended network is the reference.
- **Superposition in RF:** small-signal models are linear, so superposition of small signals is fine; with large signals (PA, mixer, limiter) it fails and harmonic-balance simulation is needed ([RF simulators](../16-simulation-tools/rf-circuit-simulators-harmonic-balance.md)).

## Common mistakes & misconceptions
- **"Conjugate matching wastes half the power, so matched systems are only 50 % efficient."** Only if the source resistance is a physical resistor carrying the same current. A transistor's Thévenin resistance is not a heater; a PA's efficiency is set by its class of operation and harmonic terminations.
- **"The PA should be conjugate matched for maximum power."** Maximum power from a transistor is limited by voltage/current swing, so it is load-line matched. Conjugate matching maximises *small-signal gain*, not output power.
- **"The LNA input should be conjugate matched."** It should be noise matched (to $\Gamma_{opt}$); the resulting input mismatch is often accepted or fixed with feedback/degeneration or an isolator.
- **Superposing powers.** Two sources in phase at the same frequency give $|V_1+V_2|^2$, not $|V_1|^2+|V_2|^2$ (+6 dB, not +3 dB, for equal coherent sources). Incoherent signals at different frequencies do add in power.
- **Zeroing dependent sources** when computing $Z_{th}$ of a transistor circuit — gives the wrong output impedance. Use a test source.
- **Mixing rms and peak phasors.** $P_{av}=V_\text{rms}^2/(4R_s)=V_\text{pk}^2/(8R_s)$. SPICE `.ac` magnitudes are peak unless you set them otherwise.
- **"50 Ω means matched to the antenna."** 50 Ω is a reference impedance chosen so parts from different makers interoperate; each side is designed for 50 Ω, and a match exists when the antenna actually measures near 50 Ω at the operating frequency.
